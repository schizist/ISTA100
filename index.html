02 — Five core concepts
Concept 01 — The bit

A bit is a single yes/no choice. Shannon showed any message (text, audio, video) can be broken into 1s and 0s. The letter "A" in ASCII is 01000001.

Concept 02 — Entropy
Information = surprise
predictable
surprising

"The sun rose" tells you almost nothing. "It snowed in Vegas in July" is high-entropy (unexpected), therefore genuinely informative.

Concept 03 — Redundancy
Language is mostly predictable
LANGUAGE  IS   ALSO  STRANGE

Shannon calculated English is ~75% redundant. You can strip vowels from most sentences and still read them. Less redundancy = harder to crack; more = safer to transmit.

Concept 04 — Channel capacity
Every channel has a maximum

over capacity

overflow = degraded signal

A channel carries only so many bits per second, like a pipe with a fixed diameter. Shannon gave engineers the math to measure this exactly.

Concept 05 — Error-correcting codes
Any message can be sent near-perfectly, even through noise
original msg

Shannon's most surprising result: by adding carefully designed extra bits, a message can survive even a very noisy channel with near-zero errors. This is how your phone calls stay clear, how CDs still play with scratches, and how the Voyager probe sent photos from billions of miles away.

03 — Why it still matters
Wi-Fi
Channel capacity math determines how much data your router can push per second

File compression exploits Shannon's redundancy insights to shrink data without losing it

Error-correcting codes let QR codes work even when up to 30% of the image is damaged
CDs
Error correction lets scratched discs play perfectly by reconstructing missing data on the fly
4G/5G
Mobile networks use Shannon's capacity formula to maximize data rates over radio channels
AI
Language models use entropy and probability concepts rooted directly in information theory
04 — About this visual

For this module's creative exploration, I chose to visualize information theory, the framework Claude Shannon developed in his landmark 1948 paper, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication." What drew me to this concept is how it takes something we completely take for granted, the act of sending and receiving a message, and quantifies it with mathematical precision. We talk, text, stream, and browse without ever thinking about what makes any of that possible, but Shannon showed that information itself is measurable, like mass or energy. Gertner (2012) describes Shannon's core contribution as answering a question Bell engineers had struggled with from the beginning: how fast, and how accurately, can you send a message from one place to another? Putting that idea into a visual format felt worthwhile because the concepts behind it (bits, entropy, noise, channel capacity) are invisible in daily life but are quietly running underneath almost every piece of technology we use.
