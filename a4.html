02. Five core concepts
Concept 01: The bit
The fundamental unit of information

A bit is a single yes/no choice. Shannon showed any message can be broken into 1s and 0s. The letter “A” in ASCII is 01000001.

Concept 02: Entropy
Information = surprise
predictable
surprising

“It snowed in Vegas in July” is high-entropy, unexpected and therefore genuinely informative.

Concept 03: Redundancy
Language is mostly predictable
LANGUAGE IS  ALSO STRANGE

Shannon calculated English is ~75% redundant. You can strip vowels from most sentences and still read them.

Concept 04: Channel capacity
Every channel has a maximum
within limit
over capacity

A channel carries only so many bits per second. Shannon gave engineers the math to measure this exactly.

Concept 05: Error-correcting codes
Any message can be sent near-perfectly, even through noise
original msg
+
extra parity bits
→
msg survives noise

By adding carefully designed extra bits, a message can survive even a noisy channel with near-zero errors. This is how your phone calls stay clear, how CDs play with scratches, and how Voyager sent photos from billions of miles away.

03. Why it still matters
Wi-Fi
Channel capacity math determines how much data your router can push per second
ZIP
File compression exploits Shannon’s redundancy insights to shrink data losslessly
QR
Error-correcting codes let QR codes work even when 30% of the image is damaged
CDs
Error correction lets scratched discs play perfectly by reconstructing missing data
4G/5G
Mobile networks use Shannon’s capacity formula to maximize data rates
AI
Language models use entropy and probability concepts rooted in information theory
04. About this visual

For this module’s creative exploration, I chose to visualize information theory, the framework Claude Shannon developed in his landmark 1948 paper, “A Mathematical Theory of Communication.” What drew me to this concept is how it takes something we completely take for granted, the act of sending and receiving a message, and quantifies it with mathematical precision. Gertner (2012) describes Shannon’s core contribution as answering a question Bell engineers had struggled with from the beginning: how fast, and how accurately, can you send a message from one place to another?
