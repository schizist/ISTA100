How Bell Labs Sparked the Greatest Era of American Innovation

The Information Age

The information age is marked by the transition from analog to digital technologies, enabling unprecedented access to and sharing of information. This new age was heralded forth in the 1940s with the invention of the transistor at Bell Labs which in turn enabled the development of modern computers, smartphones, and the internet, ultimately ushering in the Information Age.

Modern industrial research is meant to apply science to the 'common affairs' of everyday life. It is an instrument capable of avoiding many of the mistakes of a blind cut-and-try experimentation. It is likewise an instrument which can bring to bear an aggregate of creative force on any particular problem which is infinitely greater than any force which can be conceived of as residing in the intellectual capacity of an individual.
— Frank Jewett, President of Bell Labs

Bell Labs: The Idea Factory

The Vision of Theodore Vail

Theodore Vail, who became AT&T's president in 1907, transformed the struggling phone company with a revolutionary philosophy: "One policy, one system, universal service." He argued that telephone service had become "necessary to existence" and that the public would be best served by a technologically unified and compatible system.

Vail understood that government regulation was necessary for monopoly protection, but in return, AT&T deserved reasonable profits and industry dominance. Most importantly, he defined AT&T as a technological leader with legions of engineers working unceasingly to improve the system. This strategy measured the company's progress "in decades instead of years."

The Transcontinental Challenge

In 1909, Frank Jewett and John J. Carty traveled to San Francisco to determine how to provide transcontinental phone service—New York to San Francisco—in time for the Panama-Pacific International Exposition. The crux of the problem was a satisfactory telephone repeater or amplifier. As Jewett later recalled their late-night debates:

Did we know how to develop such a repeater? No. Why not? Science hadn't yet shown us the way. Did we have any reason to think that she would? Yes. In time? Possibly.
— Frank Jewett, reflecting on the transcontinental telephone challenge

This challenge led to a new approach: rather than relying solely on engineers, they turned to scientists. Harold Arnold, a physicist from Millikan's lab, improved Lee De Forest's audion tube (soon to be known as the vacuum tube), which would revolutionize twentieth-century communications. The transcontinental line, completed in 1915, consisted of four copper wires strung coast-to-coast over 130,000 wooden poles.
